# 2025 *In Arms et Sine Arms* Harnischfechten — event account

> **Status:** Secondary event account, not an official ruleset. The rules were provided verbally, so details may be uncertain.

## Source

Grauenwolf, “Rules for Harnischfechten in HEMA,” 23 September 2025.
[Original article](https://grauenwolf.wordpress.com/2025/09/23/rules-for-harnischfechten-in-hema/)

Harnischfechten, literally “fencing in armor”, was a very important part of historical martial arts but largely ignored today. One of the most common reasons is the belief that you cannot perform historic armored techniques in a safe manner because they are designed to avoid the armor. Yet we’re learning that’s a myth and there are ways they did it which we can emulate.

What follows are the harnischfechten rules from the 2025 WMA Workshop event In Arms et Sine Arms. I witnessed this event and saw a lot of examples of fencing that looked just like the illustrations in the manuscripts. And there were accompanying classes and lectures on these techniques.

Please note that the rules were provided to me verbally. I did ask a lot of clarifying questions, but it is possible that I misunderstood some of the finer details.

## Armor

Most participants were in full harness. Some were missing the greaves, in which case fighting at the barrier was recommended.

Helmets were classified as “perforated” or “pierced”. Perforated referred to perforated sheet metal where as pierced means plain sheet metal that was drilled or punched. However, that doesn’t reflect how the rule was actually applied. Instead, think of it this way.
- A perforated helm is one that would historically be open-faced. A sheet of perforated metal is added to the front for safety.
- A pierced helm is one that would historically have a visor. And that visor may have small holes to assist with vison or breathing.

Chainmail is highly recommended for all openings.

Unarmored HEMA gear such as fencing masks are explicitly not allowed. However, unarmored longsword gauntlets are suggested as a low-cost alternative to steel gauntlets for beginners. The understanding is that you start with them, then replace the gauntlets after you’ve built the rest of your kit.

## Weapons

All weapons were provided by the venue in matched sets other than daggers.

The sword was aluminum with a hard rubber cross guard to allow for the mordhau.

The spear head was steel with an additional rubber blunt.

I believe poleax head was hard rubber, but I didn’t get a good look at it.

Daggers were provided by the participants. The blade was steel or aluminum with a minimum size to avoid going into the holes in the visors.

The messers appeared to be steel. These were only used for an exhibition match between instructors before the field opened to challengers.

## Rule Selection

This was a pas d’armes style tournament with the five instructors defending the field.

On their turn, the challenger was permitted to select the weapon they wished to use: Dagger, Sword, Poleax, or Spear. The challenger then stated if they wanted to fight at the barrier or the open field. Finally, they were allowed to optionally add the mordhau (sword only) and/or grappling.

The ceremony was as follows:
- Challenger approaches the barrier in full kit and strikes a shield with the weapon of choice.
- The challenger then strikes the barrier if it is to be used.
- The challenger loudly says “mordhau” if they want to allow that strike.
- The challenger loudly says “grips” if they want to allow grappling. (This is assumed for dagger.)
- The defenders choose amongst themselves who will answer the challenge.

Note that regardless of weapon choice, daggers are permitted as a secondary weapon.

## Basic Rules

Each challenge consists of 3 passes. All three passes are fought unless a “fight ending” condition is met.

A pass is concluded if a successful strike is made as follows:
- A solid blow to any location with a poleax or the cross guard of the sword.
- A solid thrust to a location covered by chainmail with any weapon.

Glancing blows not counted. This doesn’t mean ‘full power’ blows are required. You are expected to control your weapon to avoid causing a concussion while still maintaining accuracy.

Thrusts much be held; you can’t just tap with the point and retreat like you would with a first-blood duel.

The haft of a polearms may be used as a distraction but it doesn’t score.

Doubles are considered to be a tie. The pass is not refought. After-blows are not counted.

The fight is concluded if one of the following happens.
- A blow is struck to an unarmored location.
- A blow is struck to the face of a “perforated” helm.
- A participant is completely disarmed.
- A participant is driven from the field.

## Disarms

Note that “disarmed” means losing all weapons. If a participant loses their primary weapon but still has a dagger they are considered to be armed, even if they have not drawn their dagger.

You are allowed to draw your opponent’s dagger. There were a couple of attempts to do so when the participant’s own dagger was in an inconvenient location.

Lost weapons are not automatically returned after a pass. However, in every case other than total disarmament the opponent permitted the weapon to be recovered.

## Grappling

In theory a clean throw, in which one participant’s hand or side touches the ground and the other remains standing, would be a fight ender. But to do field conditions they decided that completed throws would not be used so I couldn’t observe it in practice.

## Judging

Two marshals judged the matches, call blows as they see them. Participants may also self-call blows.

Marshals may strike the fencers on the head to get their attention. This is necessary because the helmets limit their hearing.

## Pay to Win

Some would argue that this style of fencing encourages “pay to win” behavior. The person with the better armor, specifically smaller gaps, would have a significant advantage. And they would be right. In many exchanges, even small features on the armor such as a rolled edge on a breastplate that catches thrusts prevented several otherwise scoring blows to the armpit. And obviously a rondel would be beneficial.

However, this criticism should be tempered with the acknowledgement that the same can be said of most unarmored styles as well. A sword that is longer, stiffer, or better balanced will tend to give an advantage to its user. And everyone agrees they fence better in well fitting gear, which often means more expensive or custom-tailored jackets and gloves.

## Field

The field was open with assistant referees marking the edges with staves. No ropes were setup, presumably to avoid causing a tripping hazard.

If you look through the historic illustrations, you’ll often find the field marked with what appears to be a sand or chalk ring in lieu of a fence. That wasn’t an option during the event due it the need to clean-up the rented space.

## Barrier

The barrier is literally just a free-standing fence. The top of the barrier reached the base of the breast plate for the taller participants.

Participants could reach over the barrier with their arm to strike.

Striking through/below the barrier was not permitted.

I only observed one fight at the barrier, which used spears. It was interesting to see the tactics involved. Each participant has to decide how close to approach, closing to gain range at the expense of making themselves more exposed.

## Misc.

One of the defenders was injured, so he requested a challenger to take his place at defending the field. My understanding this was a pre-existing shoulder injury that was aggravated, not a new injury.

The daggers were used a lot.

Swords were frequently used in both the longsword (both hands on handle) and half-sword/shortened sword (one hand on blade, the other on handle) configurations. Longsword was usually used for an opening action of the more aggressive participant, transitioning to half-swording for follow-up actions.

There was no overall winner. Each match was evaluated on its own terms, not as part of a total score. In discussions, the participants felt this led to safer and more enjoyable fencing over a traditional tournament format where the focus on winning the big prize often leads to improper behavior.
